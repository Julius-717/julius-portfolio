<p className="lead">This page describes the method only. It deliberately says nothing about what the review found.</p>

## Context

An account-wide IAM least-privilege review of a production AI platform on AWS, complementing SOC 2 work on IAM hygiene: no directly attached user policies, access-key age controls, and replacing static credentials.

## Problem

Hygiene controls catch the obvious issues. They don't tell you what any given principal can *actually* do once every policy, trust relationship and boundary is taken into account, and that is the question least privilege has to answer.

## Risk

The risks in IAM are rarely single statements. They come from combinations: a broad action here, a missing condition there, a trust policy that lets one role become another. A review that reads policies one at a time, or that reports issues without confirming them, either misses these or produces noise that nobody can act on.

## Approach

The review runs in four stages and is **read-only throughout**. Nothing in the account is changed to perform it.

**1. Collect (read-only).**

- Account authorization details: users, groups, roles, and every managed and inline policy
- The credential report
- Service-last-accessed data
- IAM Access Analyzer findings

**2. Analyse offline.** The collected data is analysed away from the account, statement by statement, for known risk patterns:

| Check | What it looks for |
| --- | --- |
| Wildcards | `*` in actions or resources where something narrower is possible |
| `NotAction` | Allow statements defined by exclusion, which grant more than they appear to |
| Unconditioned `iam:PassRole` | Passing roles without constraining which role or to which service |
| Escalation primitives | Actions known to let a principal raise its own privileges |
| Trust policies | Who can assume what, and under which conditions |
| Permission boundaries | Where they apply, and whether they actually bound anything |

**3. Confirm with the IAM policy simulator.** Static analysis only produces *suspicions*. Each suspected path is tested with the IAM policy simulator before it counts as a finding, so nothing is reported on the strength of a pattern match alone.

<Illustrative>

```bash
# Illustrative: confirming a suspected path with the policy simulator.
# Placeholders only; this does not change anything in the account.
aws iam simulate-principal-policy \
  --policy-source-arn "<principal-under-review>" \
  --action-names "<action-to-test>" \
  --resource-arns "<resource-to-test>" \
  --query 'EvaluationResults[].{action:EvalActionName,decision:EvalDecision}'
```

</Illustrative>

**4. Granted vs. used.** For high-risk roles, a follow-up analysis compares what is *granted* with what is *used*, using IAM Access Advisor and CloudTrail. The difference is the candidate set for removal, backed by evidence rather than guesswork.

## Validation

Simulation is the validation step: every reported finding has been confirmed by the IAM policy simulator. The granted-vs-used stage adds a second, independent kind of evidence for the roles where it matters most.

## Outcome

A repeatable, evidence-backed review method, with every finding confirmed by simulation before it was reported.

## Lessons

- **Collect once, analyse many times.** Working offline from a read-only snapshot makes the analysis repeatable and keeps the review from changing what it is reviewing.
- **A pattern match is a lead, not a finding.** Confirmation by simulation is what makes a report worth acting on.
- **"Granted" and "used" are different questions.** Least privilege needs both answered.
